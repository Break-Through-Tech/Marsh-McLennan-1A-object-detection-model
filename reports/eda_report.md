## 6. The consumer-vs-workplace domain gap

The model trains on consumer-product injuries but will be tested on workers'-compensation narratives. That gap is the single largest risk in the project, and it is measurable.

#### Incident location

| code | location | cases | share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Not recorded | 147,425 | 35.94% |
| 1 | Home | 150,763 | 36.75% |
| 2 | Farm/Ranch | 173 | 0.04% |
| 4 | Street or highway | 8,602 | 2.10% |
| 5 | Other public property | 32,451 | 7.91% |
| 6 | Mobile/manufactured home | 100 | 0.02% |
| 7 | Industrial | 30 | 0.01% |
| 8 | School/daycare | 16,416 | 4.00% |
| 9 | Place of recreation or sports | 54,241 | 13.22% |

- Cases coded **Industrial**: **30** (0.01%)
- Narratives mentioning work explicitly ("at work", "workplace", "on the job"): **69** (0.02%)

**This is by design, not a data error.** Public NEISS is a *consumer product* surveillance system and largely excludes occupational injuries. Occupational cases live in **NEISS-Work**, the separate NIOSH occupational supplement, which the project brief lists as a stretch goal.

#### But is the *label space* also missing, or only the examples?

The `Location` field above understates how usable this data is. The NEISS product taxonomy turns out to cover workplace objects well - what is thin is the number of training examples, not the vocabulary.

| workplace object | codes in taxonomy | cases in this file |
|---|---|---|
| Fork Lift | 1 | 0 |
| Scaffold | 1 | 119 |
| Ladder | 7 | 3,915 |
| Nail Gun | 1 | 193 |
| Welding | 4 | 238 |
| Chain Saw | 1 | 518 |
| Power Saw | 4 | 507 |
| Power Drill | 3 | 206 |
| Grinder | 9 | 711 |
| Sander | 1 | 40 |
| Air Compressor | 1 | 47 |
| Tractor | 3 | 213 |
| Hoist | 2 | 552 |
| Conveyor | 1 | 0 |
| Pallet | NONE | - |
| Crane | NONE | - |

Read that table in two columns, because they say different things:

- **`codes in taxonomy`** - the label space is in good shape. Only PALLET and CRANE have no code at all. Ladders get seven codes, grinders nine. NEISS can express most workers'-comp answers.
- **`cases in this file`** - the *examples* are thin to absent. Ladders are fine at ~3,900. But FORK LIFT and CONVEYOR have a code and **zero cases in this year**, and sanders, air compressors and scaffolding sit in the dozens. You cannot learn a class from zero examples no matter how good the model is.

So this is mostly a **training-distribution** problem rather than a **label-space** problem, which is the better of the two to have: a missing label space would mean the taxonomy cannot express the answer and the project needs a different target, whereas a skewed distribution has ordinary fixes. The zero-case rows are the real warning, and they are the concrete argument for pooling years or getting NEISS-Work.

Mitigations, in rough order of effort:

1. **Evaluate on a workplace-like slice**, not a random split, so the validation number tracks the real target rather than flattering it with bedroom falls.
2. **Oversample or reweight** the tool, machinery and structural codes identified above.
3. **Pool several years** of NEISS. Rare workplace codes gain roughly linearly with each year added, at no annotation cost.
4. **Obtain NEISS-Work** and treat it as the primary training set - the highest-value option, and the one that needs the advisor's help.

## 7. Class imbalance in the target

The target is `Product_1`: **767** distinct classes in this file.

- Largest class: **FLOORS OR FLOORING MATERIALS** (47,267 cases, 11.52% of the data)
- Smallest class: **1 case(s)**
- Imbalance ratio: **47,267:1**

| class size | classes | cases covered |
|---|---|---|
| 1 case only | 28 | 28 |
| 2-4 cases | 66 | 189 |
| 5-19 cases | 156 | 1,608 |
| 20-99 cases | 211 | 10,099 |
| 100-999 cases | 237 | 81,703 |
| 1000+ cases | 69 | 316,574 |

#### What this rules out

Stratified k-fold requires at least k examples of every class. That is not satisfied here:

| scheme | classes with too few examples | cases in those classes | % of data at risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-fold stratified | 57 | 86 | 0.02% |
| 5-fold stratified | 94 | 217 | 0.05% |
| 10-fold stratified | 167 | 690 | 0.17% |

The good news is in the last column: the classes that break stratification are collectively **well under 1% of the data**. So the fix is cheap - you are not throwing away signal, you are tidying up a tail that could never have been learned from one year anyway.

**Recommended handling, in order of preference:**

1. **Merge** classes below a threshold (say 20 cases) into an `OTHER` bucket. Keeps every row, makes stratification legal, and `OTHER` becomes an honest "I don't know" class that is genuinely useful in production.
2. **Report macro-F1 twice** - once over all classes, once over only classes above the threshold. The gap tells your advisor how much of the score is tail noise.
3. **Do not simply drop** rare rows without saying so. Dropping the hard cases and reporting the resulting accuracy is the single easiest way to produce a number that does not survive contact with the test set.

## 8. Hidden confounders

Three things in this data correlate with the label but are not the text's meaning. Each one can inflate a validation score.

### 8a. Hospital (`PSU`) - the big one

NEISS narratives are written by abstractors at **77** hospitals, and house style varies enormously:

| style marker | min hospital | median hospital | max hospital |
|---|---|---|---|
| mean narrative length (chars) | 49 | 132 | 265 |
| uses 'DX:' marker | 0.00% | 99.08% | 100.00% |
| uses 'C/O' shorthand | 0.00% | 0.16% | 99.92% |

Read the `DX:` row carefully: **some hospitals use that marker on every single case and others never use it at all.** Same for `C/O`. Average narrative length varies more than fivefold between sites.

This matters because of *how the project will be graded*. The final test set is Marsh's workers'-compensation narratives - a completely different source with its own conventions. A model validated on a random split has seen every hospital's style during training, so its score says nothing about transferring to a new writing style. That is exactly the thing the project needs to predict.

Hospitals also differ in *what* they see. Total-variation distance between one hospital's label mix and the national mix:

- min **0.12**, median **0.28**, max **0.63** (0 = identical, 1 = no overlap)

And case volume is very unevenly distributed, which constrains *how* you can split by hospital:

| group | cases | share of data |
|---|---|---|
| largest hospital | 30,768 | 7.50% |
| top 5 hospitals | 100,180 | 24.42% |
| top 10 hospitals | 158,819 | 38.72% |
| smallest 20 hospitals | 12,741 | 3.11% |

**Consequence:** holding out a random 20% *of hospitals* could yield anywhere from ~2% to ~50% of the rows. Hospitals must be allocated to folds by case count, not at random. Section 10 does this.

| BICYCLES AND ACCESSORIES, (EXCL.MOUNTA | 10,456 | 7 | 12.18% | 1.5x |

**Consequence:** do **not** use a chronological split (train on Jan-Sep, test on Oct-Dec). It would put almost no swimming or baseball in the test set and an unrepresentative pile of football, measuring the calendar rather than the model. Split randomly or by hospital, and if you later pool multiple years, stratify by month.

### 8c. Duplicate narratives

- Rows whose narrative text is not unique: **1,861 (0.45%)**, across **759** distinct texts
- Of those texts, **752** carry a single label and **7** carry **conflicting** labels

The volume is too small to distort a split much, but deduplicate across the train/validation boundary anyway - it is one line of code and removes a free-points effect.

The conflicting cases are more interesting, because they put a **ceiling on achievable accuracy**: identical text, different gold label, so no model can get both right.

  - `14MOM ROLLED OFF THE BED TO A HARDWOOD FLOOR ONTO HEAD DX CLOSED HEAD INJURY`
    labelled both: **BEDS OR BEDFRAMES, OTHER OR NOT SPEC** / **FLOORS OR FLOORING MATERIALS**
  - `3YOM STUCK A BEAD IN HIS RIGHT NOSTRIL DX: FOREIGN OBJECT NOSE`
    labelled both: **ART OR CERAMICS SUPPLIES AND EQUIPME** / **JEWELRY**
  - `5YOF WITH A FOREIGN BODY IN HER NOSE - A BEAD. DX: FOREIGN BODY IN NOSE`
    labelled both: **JEWELRY** / **TOYS, NOT ELSEWHERE CLASSIFIED**

The first is a genuinely ambiguous event (rolled off a bed onto a floor - which object caused the injury?). Expect a few points of irreducible error from this kind of coder disagreement, and say so when you report results rather than chasing it.

## 10. Propose split and sampling recipe

Putting Sections 7-9 together, for the task **`Narrative_1` -> `Product_1`**.

### Step 1 - Collapse the unlearnable tail

Merge every `Product_1` class with fewer than **20 cases** into a single `OTHER` class. This affects 250 classes but only 0.44% of rows, and it is what makes stratification legal at all.

### Step 2 - Split by hospital

Assign **whole hospitals** to folds, so no hospital's writing style appears in both training and validation.

Because hospital sizes are so uneven (Section 8a), assign them largest-first to whichever fold is furthest below target rather than at random.

| fold | hospitals | cases | share | distinct products |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| train | 56 | 286,946 | 69.95% | 756 |
| val | 10 | 61,575 | 15.01% | 656 |
| test | 11 | 61,680 | 15.04% | 659 |

Label-mix drift from the national distribution (total-variation distance): train **0.022**, val **0.118**, test **0.089**. Low values mean the grouped split did not accidentally skew the labels.

Venue mix across folds - the Section 9 diagnostic, computed on rows where `Location` is recorded:

| fold | Home | Recreation/sports | Public property | School | Street |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| train | 54.37% | 21.97% | 13.29% | 6.59% | 3.68% |
| val | 68.34% | 13.09% | 12.98% | 3.20% | 2.25% |
| test | 56.98% | 23.96% | 7.72% | 8.40% | 2.81% |

**This table shows the real cost of splitting by hospital.** The label mix stays close to national (the drift figures above are small), but the *venue* mix does not: **Home** differs by **14 percentage points** between folds. Hospitals serve different catchments - a paediatric or suburban site sees a different mix of home and sports injuries than an inner-city one - and holding out whole sites inherits that.

This is a trade-off:

- A **grouped** split buys independence from writing style and pays for it with venue drift.
- A **stratified random** split holds venue and labels steady and pays for it by letting house style leak.


### Step 3 - Also keep a stratified random split, as a control

Build a second, ordinary split stratified on `Product_1`. Train the same model on both.

- The **stratified** score is your optimistic ceiling.
- The **grouped-by-hospital** score is your realistic estimate for Marsh's data.
- **The gap between them is a result in its own right** - it quantifies how much your model leans on house style rather than on the description of the object. That comparison is worth a slide on its own in the November presentation.

### Step 4 - Balance exposure during training

- **Fine-tuned / classical classifier:** use class weights in the loss (`class_weight='balanced'`, or `1/sqrt(frequency)` which is less aggressive and usually behaves better across 700+ classes), or a class-balanced batch sampler. Either gives FLOORS and NAIL GUNS comparable influence per step without inventing data.
- **LLM prompting:** "balancing" means **few-shot exemplar selection**. Draw exemplars stratified across product groups, not from the head of the distribution, or the model will anchor on floors and beds. Keep the exemplar pool strictly inside the training fold.

### Step 5 - Deduplicate across the fold boundary

Drop exact narrative duplicates that would otherwise straddle train and validation. Small effect here (0.45% of rows) but free to do.
